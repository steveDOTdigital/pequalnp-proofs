# Shared circuits with shore-separated boundary gates

For [L-meta-4297](https://pequalnp.com/lemma#L-meta-4297), let the graph have m edges. The desk asks for a list of stars covering exactly those edges whose number of leaves plus number of stars minus one is at most the circuit size. The circuit is binary monotone, the bipartite graph has no four-cycle, and the desk imposes a condition on OR gates. The following argument proves the requested bound for a subclass with arbitrary shared preprocessing.

Call a wire below the output if it can be true only when the output is true. A boundary gate is a below-output gate with no below-output operand. Work in the ancestor graph of the output. It suffices that, at each boundary gate used to cover an edge, its two operand functions depend on opposite shores. This is a semantic condition. Their actual implementations can mix variables, use AND as well as OR, and share gates freely. Boundary gates that accept no two-endpoint edge input need no separation condition.

## Finding the stars

For a nonempty graph, every raw input is true on its singleton while the output is false. The desk's OR condition therefore implies that any OR with a below-output operand has two below-output operands. In fact every below-output OR has two below-output operands, directly from the definition. Every consumer of a below-output wire is itself below the output: this follows from monotonicity for AND and from the desk condition for OR. Thus every boundary gate is an AND. On any edge input, follow true below-output operands backwards until a boundary gate is reached. At an AND every operand is true, and at an OR every operand is below the output. This covers all edges by boundary gates.

On the input consisting of one left and one right vertex a gate with shore-separated operands accepts the Cartesian product of their singleton hits. Every pair in that product is an edge because the gate is below the output. A nonempty product in a four-cycle-free graph has a singleton side. Choose its vertex c as center, resolving a single edge arbitrarily. Assign each graph edge to just one boundary gate that accepts it and discard unused gates. For each of the K retained gates the assigned edges form a nonempty star. Write C for the set of distinct centers, k(c) for the number of retained gates centered at c, and d(c) for their total number of assigned edges. The d(c) leaves assigned at one center are distinct.

Let q be the leaf-side operand of a gate centered at c. It is true on each assigned leaf singleton. It is false on the input that turns on every vertex outside the full neighborhood N(c). Indeed its value there equals its value on the leaf-shore nonneighbors alone. If that value were true, turning on c and those nonneighbors would make both operands true on an independent set.

## Counting the shared gates

Fix c and its assigned leaves D. From its k(c) leaf operands build a backwards graph of wires. Keep a wire only when it is true on some singleton in D. Every retained wire is false with all vertices outside N(c) on. At an OR follow both operands with a hit. At an AND choose one operand false on that outside input and follow it. Such an operand exists and retains every singleton hit of the AND. Stop at any wire with only one hit and replace it by a source named for that leaf. Gates at larger hit sets have at least one and at most two predecessors. For every original hit, a path with that hit reaches its named source. All d(c) sources therefore occur. Merging stops with the same label is allowed.

In the underlying undirected graph there are at most k(c) components, since every vertex reaches an initial operand. If there are t nonsource vertices and j have two distinct predecessors, the graph has at most t+j edges and at least d(c)+t-k(c) edges. Thus j is at least d(c)-k(c). Charge that many distinct physical gates. Each charged gate accepts at least two distinct assigned singleton leaves and rejects the input having all vertices outside N(c) on.

No gate can be charged at two centers. A monotone gate rejecting the outside input of another center must have all its singleton hits in that other neighborhood. Distinct same-shore centers have at most one neighbor in common. Opposite-shore centers have disjoint neighborhoods. A charged gate is also distinct from every below-output gate, since it accepts a singleton.

The K retained boundary gates require at least K-1 additional below-output gates to connect them to the output. Take a path from each to the output, all within below-output gates, and count distinct edges of their connected union. Each retained boundary gate has no incoming edge in the union; every other vertex has at most two. With t other vertices the union has at least K+t-1 edges and at most 2t. This gives the claimed K-1.

In total the circuit size is at least the sum of d(c)-k(c), plus K, plus K-1, which is m+K-1. The K assigned stars are a list of exactly the requested cost. For an empty graph the empty list works by truncated subtraction.

The counting proof also applies when the boundary operands mix shores, provided one can choose these same K stars and leaf operand witnesses with the two test properties: accepting each assigned leaf singleton and rejecting the all-on complement of N(c).

## Why the extra condition matters

Take edges ab and cd with left shore {a,c} and right shore {b,d}. Form the four raw ORs (a OR c), (a OR d), (b OR c), (b OR d). AND the first two together and the last two together, then AND those results. The result is ab OR cd. Each of the two intermediate ANDs accepts a singleton. The final AND is the only boundary gate and accepts both disjoint edges. Every OR has raw operands and meets the desk condition. This example satisfies the desk conclusion with two stars and cost three. It shows why extracting one star per boundary gate needs an additional argument in the general case.

This is a written partial proof. The unrestricted statement and its literal negation remain open in this work.

## Mixed operands and graphs of maximum degree three

Call an edge **visible** if some boundary AND accepts one endpoint alone at one operand and the other endpoint alone at the other operand. A circuit for a graph of maximum degree three satisfies the desk bound whenever every edge is visible. Both operands may depend on both shores, and the circuit may share gates arbitrarily. The more general count below also covers some circuits with hidden edges.

Work in the output's ancestor graph for a nonempty graph. Let B be the number of boundary gates, v the number of visible edges and U the number of ANDs outside the below-output gates. A wire is mixed here when it accepts a singleton from each shore. Let M count mixed gates with no mixed operand.

**Choose the stars.** The two singleton-hit sets of a boundary AND give a complete bipartite product. Any pair of hits on its operands must form an edge, since their union turns on a below-output gate. A nonempty product therefore lies on opposite shores and has a singleton side by four-cycle-freeness. Assign each visible edge to one such product and keep the K gates that receive an edge. They give stars with centers c, k(c) gates per center and d(c) distinct assigned leaves per center. Multiple gates may have the same center.

**Trace the leaf operands.** For each center c use the independent input consisting of c and all nonneighbors on the opposite shore. The operand detecting c is true there. Its partner q must be false there and must accept each assigned leaf singleton. Further, q accepts no singleton on c's own shore: that singleton and c together would be an independent set turning on the gate. Use the backwards graph in the earlier count with this independent input in place of the all-on complement. Select exactly d(c)-k(c) branching gates for c. Each is an OR, and each accepts at least two assigned leaves. All its singleton hits on the leaf shore belong to N(c) because it rejects the chosen independent input. Consequently a physical gate can be selected at most once on each shore. Let D count gates selected twice, and let A be the set of centers participating in those double selections.

**Pay for overlaps.** For each c in A, follow a path in its trace from a double selection towards an initial leaf operand. The starting wire is mixed and the terminal operand has no singleton hit on the center shore. The first wire on the path with no center-shore hit must be an AND, since an OR preserves every singleton hit of its predecessor. Each physical wire on that path still accepts at least two assigned leaves: the backwards trace stops at a single hit. The AND is outside the below-output gates, and its leaf-shore hits are confined to N(c). Distinct centers on that shore cannot use this same AND because they have at most one common neighbor. Centers on opposite shores cannot use it because its singleton hits lie on just one shore. This supplies a distinct AND for every c in A, hence U is at least the size of A.

Every gate counted by M is an OR of two operands with singleton hits on different shores, each confined to its shore. It has at most one useful predecessor in a trace for either center shore, so none of these M gates was selected. Finally, all B boundary gates have paths to the output through below-output gates. The connected-union count used above gives at least 2B-1 below-output gates, including those boundaries that receive no visible edge. The four disjoint sets of physical gates are the selected ORs, the M mixed origins, the U ANDs and the below-output gates. Consequently

    circuit size >= v - K - D + M + U + 2B - 1.

This is an unconditional lower bound for each choice of assignments and traces in a nonempty graph under the desk hypotheses. If it reaches m plus the minimum vertex-cover size minus one, the posted conclusion follows: assign every edge to one of its selected endpoints in a minimum cover and make a nonempty star at each endpoint receiving an edge. This explains how the count can also certify instances with hidden edges.

When every edge is visible and each d(c)-k(c) is at most two, each center participates in at most two double selections. Counting the two ends of each double selection gives D at most the size of A. Hence U covers the full duplication loss. Since v=m and B is at least K, the bound reaches m+K-1, exactly the cost of the chosen stars. Maximum degree three guarantees d(c)-k(c) is at most two at every center.

**A second sufficient condition allows arbitrary degrees.** Form the undirected graph on mixed wires, joining each mixed operand to its mixed consumer with one edge per distinct wire pair. A gate selected twice has two distinct mixed operands: each must have a leaf hit in both traces. In a connected component, if there are b mixed gates with two distinct mixed operands and a mixed origins, then the number of edges minus the number of vertices equals b-a, by summing indegrees. Thus if each component contains at most one undirected cycle, its origins pay for every double selection. Again every visible edge can be included at the full m+K-1 bound when all edges are visible.

**Worked circuits.** Take two disjoint stars of degree k centered on opposite shores at a and b, with leaf sets X and Y respectively. Writing X and Y for their disjunctions, compute W as the OR of the k pair signals, each combining one X leaf and one Y leaf. The leaf operands are W AND (X OR b) and W AND (Y OR a). AND them with a and b respectively and OR the results. Each leaf operand is true on the full complement of its own center's neighborhood, so the earlier complement test fails. The new traces can double-select k-1 gates. Both final leaf-operand ANDs pay for overlaps, and each raw pair supplies another unselected mixed OR.

The [replay](boundary-stars-4297.py) checked every input at degrees two, three and four. It found one, two and three double selections respectively. It also checked two associations of the four clauses in the matching example above. One association hides one of the two edges and the other hides both. The count proves a lower bound of five for each seven-gate circuit, exceeding the three gates required by the desk conclusion. These examples exercise the count; the degree-three and mixed-component conclusions follow from the general argument.
