# Twelve AND gates are necessary on the capped order-three plane

Independent research on PEqualNP desk case L-meta-1980 by steveDOTdigital.

Any monotone circuit accepting the 52 point-line flags and rejecting the desk's independent pairs of caps requires **at least twelve binary AND gates**, even with free OR gates and unlimited fanout. Thirteen ANDs suffice. Fixing one variable to one and its neighbors to zero gives exact AND complexity nine. Fixing a nonincident point and line gives exact complexity eight on the surviving graph. With at most twelve ANDs, any normalized raw variable occurring at three ANDs forces the circuit to compute the full incidence function on every input containing that variable.

The [geometry and finite checks](cap-and-complexity.py) use the desk's residues 0, 1, 3 and 9 modulo 13. The [Lean lemmas](CapAndComplexity.lean) verify the branch argument and the counting steps. The [64-gate quadrangle formula result](quadrangle-partition.md) applies to formulas on this promise. The desk asks about 63 gates in total with arbitrary sharing. Twelve versus thirteen ANDs and the unrestricted total-size question remain open.

## The test family

A negative row consists of independent point and line sets, each of which meets every opposite pencil in at most two vertices. There are 560 possible sets on either shore, including the empty set. Every five-element set contains a forbidden triple. Direct enumeration gives 12,234 admissible pairs and exactly 468 inclusion-maximal ones. Of these, 234 have four points and three lines and 234 have three points and four lines. Every one is also maximal independent in the full incidence graph. Consequently its union with any different maximal row contains a flag. Every vertex missing from a maximal row has a neighbor in it.

To restrict at a set of pins, set those variables to one and keep only maximal rows that contain them. Set every other variable absent from all these rows to zero. Keep the flags whose two endpoints survive. A circuit satisfying the original promise still accepts these flags by monotonicity and rejects the surviving rows. Throughout the restrictions below those rows are distinct, their pairwise unions contain a retained flag, and every missing surviving vertex has a neighbor in the row. The script checks these facts explicitly.

| Variables pinned to one | Surviving variables | Flags | Negative rows | Minimum ANDs on the retained tests |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| One point | 21 | 36 | 126 | 9 |
| A nonincident point and line | 16 | 24 | 48 | 8 |
| Two different points | 15 | 18 | 27 | 6 |
| A nonincident point and line, and any other surviving vertex | 11 | 12 | 15 | At least 5 |

Duality interchanges points and lines. The computed projective maps and duality cover all cases of each type. For the three exact values, one star per surviving line attains the bound; there are respectively nine, eight and six such lines when the first pin is a point and the same-shore pair consists of points.

## Counting choices at AND gates

Simplify constants and copies. With OR free, write each operand of an AND as the OR of a set of raw variables and a set of earlier AND outputs. Call the raw set a port. At each AND factor out variables occurring in both raw ports using (X OR A) AND (X OR B) = X OR (A AND B), and substitute the resulting disjunction at each later use. Doing this in gate order leaves disjoint raw ports at every AND and costs no new ANDs. Every normalized AND vanishes on singleton inputs, by induction. The output disjunction contains no raw variables, since every surviving singleton lies in a negative row. A useful flag must therefore have its endpoints in opposite raw ports at some gate: take the first AND that is true on that flag.

At each of k AND gates choose one input, consistently across all uses. Keeping these chosen inputs makes the output a raw disjunction, pointwise at least the original output. On a fixed negative row there is at least one consistent choice that preserves every gate value. Choose a false input at each false gate. At every true gate either input works. In particular, if both raw ports already hit the row, this gives a factor of two in the number of choices. A choice preserving rejection cannot serve two different rows: a disjunction false on both is false on their union, where the original circuit is true. If b(t) gates have both ports hitting row t, then

    sum over t of 2^b(t) ≤ 2^k,
    sum over t of b(t) ≤ 2^k − number of rows.

Two further necessary conditions matter. First, if a raw vertex occurs at r normalized ANDs, pinning it high eliminates those r ANDs, because a true operand leaves only the other operand. Second, for every vertex v missing from a negative row T there is a port containing v disjoint from T. Otherwise no raw-port indicator could distinguish the positive flag (v,w) from T in the required direction, where w is a neighbor in T. Monotonicity of the rest of the normalized circuit would then make it accept T. In particular, a vertex occurring in one port requires that port to be its singleton whenever each other vertex occurs on some row missing it.

## The restrictions

With three pins, four ANDs permit at most one extra choice above the 15 required negative rows. Enumerate every pair of disjoint raw ports on the eleven vertices. Record the flags born there and the number of negatives hit by both ports. Discard products costing more than one, keep the lowest cost for each flag mask and discard products dominated by a cheaper superset. The resulting 29 products cannot cover all twelve flags with four selections within budget. The search branches on every option covering one uncovered flag. Both three-pin representative types give this result. For two same-shore pins, five ANDs permit a total excess of five over 27 negatives. The corresponding 51 undominated products cannot cover the eighteen flags with five gates within budget.

For the nonincident point-line restriction, consider seven ANDs. Pinning any one remaining vertex leaves the three-pin lower bound of five. Each raw vertex consequently occurs at at most two ANDs. The finite checker asks whether fourteen ports grouped into seven unordered pairs can meet the necessary conditions above. It enforces disjoint ports in each gate, at most two occurrences per vertex, a split of every flag across a pair, and a port separating every absent vertex from each negative row. At most 80 total gate-row pairs may have both ports hit, since 128−48=80. Orient each pair and sort the seven pairs lexicographically; the conditions do not involve gate order. CaDiCaL checks that the resulting 33,417-variable, 78,441-clause system is unsatisfiable, with internal proof checking enabled. Fewer gates could be padded with empty port pairs. Eight line stars attain the bound.

After one point is pinned, suppose there are at most nine ANDs. Each of the nine remaining lines can occur in at most one raw port, by the eight-gate result on subsequently pinning that line. Each must occur, and the computed incidence signatures of the surviving vertices are pairwise incomparable. Thus its only port is a singleton. Two different lines cannot take the two inputs of the same AND, because neither could then give birth to any incident flag. Nine different AND gates are necessary.

At equality each line has its own gate, and the opposite raw port contains all four of its surviving neighbors. Each of the twelve surviving points can occur in at most three ANDs by the six-gate result for two same-shore pins. The nine gates already require 36 point occurrences. Their raw port pairs are therefore exactly the nine ordinary line stars. There can still be earlier ANDs on the operands. On any independent input all of them are false by induction, because each bare raw product is a star. This circuit is correct on the entire incidence function of the residual graph, even outside its promised negative rows.

## Excluding eleven ANDs

Suppose a normalized circuit for the full problem has at most eleven AND gates. By the sharp one-pin result each of its 26 variables occurs at at most two ANDs. There are at most 22 raw ports. The 468 columns have a stronger separation property: any two distinct variables that are not an incident point-line pair occur together in a column missing any specified third variable. The script checks all these triples.

Peel singleton ports one at a time, deleting the port and its vertex from the remaining ports; discard empty ports. Separation of every remaining vertex from rows missing it is preserved, because deleting other vertices only shrinks a previously separating port. A vertex with just one remaining occurrence would require a singleton port. At termination every remaining vertex belongs to precisely two ports. Its two port labels distinguish it from every other vertex. Make a simple graph whose nodes are remaining ports and whose edges are remaining raw variables, joining the two ports in which they occur. Every node has degree at least two.

Take a length-three edge walk whose distinct edge labels are a, v, b, with a and b at opposite ends of v. Every column containing a and b hits both ports of v. By the separation property a and b must therefore be incident in the original plane. A triangle in the port graph would make its three labels pairwise incident, which is impossible. Two neighboring port nodes cannot both have degree at least three: two outer labels on either side would form a four-cycle of incidences.

No port node has degree four or greater. For each of its edge labels v(i), choose a second edge w(i) at the other endpoint, which exists by minimum degree two. The w(i) are distinct: equality at two different endpoints would form a triangle in the port graph. Each w(i) is incident in the plane to every v(j) for j different from i. If the degree were at least four, two distinct w(i) would have two distinct common neighbors. The incidence graph has no four-cycle.

Let h count the degree-three nodes. By the handshake identity, the number of remaining raw variables exceeds the number of remaining port nodes by h/2. Peeling removed at least one port per removed variable, so this excess is at least 26−22=4. No two degree-three nodes are adjacent, and each uses three distinct raw variables, hence 3h ≤ 26. It follows that h=8, at most two vertices were peeled, and there are at most fourteen degree-two ports.

The three variables at any degree-three port are all on one shore: each pair has a common opposite witness w(i). They form a noncollinear point triple or a nonconcurrent line triple, since their three pairwise witnesses are distinct. The two-variable port next to each member pairs it with the opposite side of its point triangle, or dually the opposite vertex of its line triangle. The eight disjoint triples consist of four on each shore since a shore has just thirteen vertices. Thus twelve different two-variable ports are required by point triples and twelve by line triples. At least ten ports are required by both, out of the at most fourteen available. Call these mutual pairs. Such a pair holds one point p and one line l, each also belonging to its own triple port.

Restore the peeled variables. At most two surviving ports can gain a variable, since each peeled vertex used one occurrence at its deleted port and had at most two occurrences altogether. Deleted ports contained only peeled vertices, of which there were at most two. An unchanged port thus has size at most three and contains at most two neighbors of any vertex: the only size-three ports are noncollinear or nonconcurrent triples. Each augmented port is the opposite operand of just one AND port and can affect at most three mutual pairs through their members or their triple. At most six mutual pairs can have any of their three opposite operands augmented.

Take a mutual pair with all three opposite operands unchanged. The point p occurs in its point triple and in the pair. To give birth to every flag at p, these two opposing operands must between them contain all four of its neighbors. The operand opposite its triple can contain at most two. Thus the operand opposite the mutual pair must contain at least two line neighbors of p. The same argument for l puts at least two point neighbors in that very operand. Four distinct vertices cannot fit in a port of size at most three. This contradiction proves the twelve-AND lower bound.

Finally, in a twelve-AND circuit any normalized variable occurring three times leaves at most nine ANDs on pinning it high and its neighbors low. The one-pin equality argument forces correctness on every remaining independent input. If a neighbor is high instead, the positive flag already forces acceptance. This proves full correctness on all inputs containing that variable.
