# Repairing AND gates without an owned edge

For the edge function of a simple graph, disabling a gate means that every reader gets zero in its place. Call an edge *pivotal at that gate* if disabling it makes the output false on the input consisting of its two endpoints. The pivotal edges can be a proper subset of the edges on which the gate itself is true.

Case L-meta-5357 asks for a least binary monotone circuit in which each AND owns an edge, under a strict upper bound on the supplied least circuit. An AND owns an edge if it is true there and **every AND strictly inside its circuit cone** is false there. Write $m=|E|$, let $\tau$ be the minimum vertex-cover size and put $U=m+\tau-1$ for a nonempty graph. The strict premise is $s<U$. For any cover, assign each edge to one of its cover endpoints and OR the resulting stars. Using a minimum cover gives a circuit of size $U$ in which every AND owns its nonempty star. Thus proving the lower bound $s\ge U$ for a family also settles the desk implication there.

The [Lean file](EdgeOwnerExchange.lean) proves an exact one-edge repair in the desk circuit model, including its strict-cone ownership and preservation of earlier owners. The mathematical results below give larger repairs, a lower bound for graphs with long subdivided feedback edges, and finite bounds for general graphs with neither triangles nor four-cycles. The desk inequality holds for every graph with a vertex cover of size at most four and every graph on at most eight active vertices. The assignment for arbitrary graphs with the strict premise remains open.

## Repairing the first gate without an owner

Let $h$ be an AND without an owned edge and let $P_h$ be its pivotal edges. In a least circuit for a nonempty edge function every live AND has a pivotal edge. Otherwise setting it to zero preserves every minimal positive input and cannot turn on a negative. Constants and dead wires can be simplified, saving a gate.

If $P_h=\{uv\}$, replace the gate at the **same index** by $x_u\wedge x_v$. An input containing no edge sees the replacement zero; the result can only decrease there. At an edge different from $uv$ the output still accepts even when the gate is forced zero. At $uv$ the replacement is true, so it preserves acceptance. These tests imply correctness on the whole Boolean cube by monotonicity. The new gate has empty strict cone and owns $uv$, irrespective of sharing and the position of the output wire.

Suppose an old AND $k\ne h$ owned an edge $e$. If $h$ was outside its cone, $k$ and every wire in its cone retain their old behavior. Otherwise $h$ was false on $e$. The replacement is also false there since $h$ was true on the pivotal edge $uv$. All wire values on $e$ are then unchanged, and the strict cone of $k$ can only shrink. Thus every old ownership survives, including ownership by later gates. The Lean theorem `normalize_unique`$ uses only the earlier-owner part: scan gates in order, repairing the first failure whenever its pivotal set is contained in one actual edge. The checker verifies that this produces a same-size correct circuit in the project's model. Consequently a failure of the desk conclusion on a nonempty graph requires a least circuit whose first ownerless AND has at least **two distinct pivotal edges**. This is a restriction on lost outputs, stronger than requiring the gate to turn on at two edges.

## Repair from earlier gates or from a private portion of the cone

For an ownerless $h$, consider the maximal independent inputs at which forcing $h$ high turns the output on. A strict ancestral AND is eligible if it is false on every such input. This is a condition on the actual output context of $h$; the ancestor can be true on independent inputs elsewhere.

Suppose one or two eligible ancestral ANDs jointly cover $P_h$, meaning that at least one accepts each pivotal edge. Replace $h$ by their OR, using a copy if there is just one. The result passes all positive edge tests for the same reason as in the one-edge proof. On a maximal independent input either the context rejects even with $h$ true, or all selected ancestors are false. Thus it rejects every negative by monotonicity. Every old owned edge survives: if $h$ lay in its owner's strict cone, then $h$ and all the selected ancestral ANDs were **already false** on that edge. The rewiring adds no new ancestors. The operation removes one ownerless AND and creates none. A least circuit minimizing their number therefore has no such gate.

Every pivotal edge of an ownerless $h$ has some owning AND strictly inside the cone of $h$. Choose the earliest true AND in that cone; its own strict ancestors lie in the same cone. In particular, if $h$ has two pivotal edges and each has an eligible owning ancestor, the two-source repair applies. A remaining two-edge obstruction must have one pivotal edge for which **every** owning ancestor accepts some sensitive maximal independent input.

There is a size tradeoff. Let $T$ be any set of graph edges containing $P_h$ on all of which $h$ was true. Replace $h$ by the OR of the stars of $T$ using only raw inputs. Its internal cost is at most $|T|+\tau(T)-1$. It fits if the old gate together with its newly dead private ancestors supplies that many gates. The replacement is below the old gate on every input, is on for every lost edge, and every new AND owns an edge. All surviving old owners keep their edges since a new AND can turn on only where the replaced gate previously did. One may combine eligible ancestral ANDs with new raw stars and pay only their actual combining and preparation gates, provided retained ancestors are excluded from the saving.

For simultaneous deletions, force a set of ownerless ANDs to zero and simplify. Let $L$ be the edges actually lost at the *output* in this joint experiment. Individual pivotal sets cannot be added to obtain $L$. Restore the OR of the lost-edge stars at the output. With nonconstant residual the additional cost is at most $|L|+\tau(L)$, including its final OR. With zero residual it is at most $|L|+\tau(L)-1$ for nonempty $L$. An old owned edge either had the changed gates outside its owner's cone or had them false there. The remaining old owners retain their edges, and the new stars own theirs. This gives a second verifiable repair criterion when a group of gates collectively frees sufficient capacity.

On a four-input matching, the formula
$$
 ((x\vee z)\wedge(x\vee w))\wedge((y\vee z)\wedge(y\vee w))
$$
has two disjoint pivotal edges $xy,zw$ at its root. Both inner ANDs are true on both edges, and each also accepts a negative on its own. Neither is an eligible replacement. The private cone does pay for restoring the matching stars. This example distinguishes the two repair criteria. The [finite checks](edge-owner-exchange.py) also exercise a circuit in which neither immediate operand is usable and two earlier eligible signals jointly repair the gate.

## Exact lower bounds for long subdivisions

Let $G$ have no triangle or four-cycle. Suppose it contains a collection of internally disjoint paths, each with at least **six consecutive internal degree-two vertices**, and the endpoints lie outside every other designated interior, and cutting the chosen run in every path leaves a forest. Equivalently, one construction starts with a simple graph, selects a feedback edge set, and replaces each selected edge by a path having at least six internal vertices. Tree attachments away from the six-vertex runs are permitted under the first formulation. Then the minimum monotone circuit size of $G$ is exactly $m+\tau-1$ when it has an edge. This allows odd cycles, arbitrary degrees at the ends and arbitrary reuse of circuit gates.

We use an elementary normalization of a least circuit. Simplify constants, invalid reads, copies and dead gates for its nonconstant output. Call a wire safe if its truth always implies a graph edge. Whenever an OR of a safe signal $W$ and an unsafe signal $A$ occurs, bypass the OR by $A$ and append $W$ to the final output disjunction. On inputs with $W=0$ the old and bypassed circuits agree. Every input with $W=1$ really is positive. This costs at most the deleted OR. For termination, keep the final disjunction as a separate list of safe terms, including the current original output. Process mixed ORs in the remaining prefix. Each step removes one prefix gate, and all previously safe terms remain safe because their values can only decrease; adding back $W$ preserves the whole output. The new final disjunction only combines safe terms. Consequently there exists a least circuit with no OR that directly mixes a safe and an unsafe operand. Further simplification and discarding dead gates preserve this property, or the process can be repeated if the circuit size drops.

Here is a local way to force multiple readers. Let $X$ consist of vertices of degree at least two. Suppose each $x\in X$ has a neighbor in $X$ and a neighbor $y$ with $N(y)\subseteq X$. Then at least one $x\in X$ has two direct readers in the normalized least circuit. Suppose all have a sole reader and take the first gate reading any of them. A raw sibling at an AND cannot be the sole companion of a degree-two-or-higher input $x$: the gate must turn on at each edge $xz$, since $x$ is essential on every such edge. A raw sibling $w$ at an OR would require $N(x)\subseteq N(w)$. Indeed if $w$ and some neighbor $z$ make an independent set (possibly $w=z$), the OR is already on and switching on $x$ cannot change the output. Two common neighbors are prohibited.

A nonraw sibling $W$ at this first reader uses only inputs outside $X$. At a sole AND it must be true on each neighbor singleton, impossible for a neighbor inside $X$, since $W$ is zero at the empty input. At a sole OR it must be unsafe by normalization. Hence there is an independent set $I$ outside $X$ that turns $W$ on. Adding $y$ keeps it independent because $N(y)\subseteq X$. Switching on $x$ must now change the output, yet the sole reader is already on. This proves the claim.

For an improved test without the neighborhood-containment assumption, the same masking argument shows that $I$ must contain a neighbor of **each** vertex in $N(x)$. No member of $I$ belongs to $N(x)$, since then $I$ itself masks an edge. Because of the four-cycle condition we can select distinct representatives $z_y\in I\cap N(y)$ for all $y\in N(x)$. These second neighbors are pairwise nonadjacent. Thus an unsafe private OR needs an independent set of distance-two representatives entirely among the inputs available to its sibling. The Lean file verifies the independent-set extension and distinctness steps.

Apply the multiple-reader result to the four middle vertices of a six-vertex run. For each of these four, one of its two neighbors has both of its neighbors among the four. Both neighbors of any candidate have degree two in the full graph. Choose a resulting multiply read vertex $x$. A zero pin saves at least three gates or a one pin saves at least four. Three readers suffice on the zero pin. For exactly two with an AND reader, the zero pin eliminates both readers and a consumer of that AND. A consumer cannot be the other direct reader by absorption. For two OR readers the one pin sets both to one and removes their consumers. If their only consumer were a common gate, factoring the common $x$ out of that gate saves an operation in the original circuit. Neither reader is the output, since the considered restriction remains nonconstant.

The zero pin deletes two edges; the cover number drops by at most one. The one pin leaves the two neighbors as separate singleton alarms and deletes the edges meeting these neighbors, at most four edges in all. The cover number drops by at most two, since adding the two neighbors covers all deleted edges. The two alarms cost two additional gates over a nonconstant residual edge function: zero them one at a time, each time removing an essential input and at least one gate. The surviving graph is nonempty since edges remain at the ends of the six-vertex run. Thus the two restricted lower targets are at least $U-3$ and $U-4$, respectively. Either gate saving pays the whole loss. Choose a designated run that still lies on a cycle. Either pin breaks that cycle and leaves the other designated runs intact. Cutting those remaining runs leaves a subgraph of a forest. Induction on the number of surviving designated cyclic runs finishes with the project's [forest bound](https://github.com/open-goat/pequalnp-proofs/blob/2ca5c823bfda02cdb19fdd17e95be8ef8028bc52/lean/PEqualNP/MetaComplexity/Helpers/ForestBound.lean). The usual star circuit meets the bound and gives an edge to every AND.

## Finite verification

The calculation grows unlabeled graphs by adding one vertex at a time. New neighbors must be at pairwise distance at least three before the vertex is added. Isomorphism uses refinement by neighbor counts followed by individualization until the partition is discrete. Equal open neighborhoods allow a branch to be skipped by the transposition of twins. Removing the last vertex gives the reverse construction for every graph. Final counts omit isolated vertices, which have no effect on the minimum.

For each residual graph the program computes the minimum cover by direct enumeration. It uses the forest bound, the [checked eleven-gate theta bound](https://github.com/open-goat/pequalnp-proofs/blob/2ca5c823bfda02cdb19fdd17e95be8ef8028bc52/lean/PEqualNP/Outside/L_meta_517.lean), and restrictions of the graph function. Duplicate leaves with a common neighbor can be removed for a one-gate lower charge. An isolated edge contributes two gates over a nonempty remaining graph: at an endpoint some controlling pin eliminates its first reader and a consumer, and both pins leave the remaining graph as a restriction. The first reader cannot itself be the whole output on the controlling pin. In a graph with no isolated edges or twin leaves the first binary raw gate cannot funnel both of its inputs through their single common reader: an AND would isolate that edge, an OR would give identical neighborhoods. Hence one raw vertex has two readers.

If a vertex has $d$ readers, $a$ of them AND, zero pins remove at least $d+\min(a,2)$ gates and one pins remove at least $d+\min(d-a,2)$. This is the same consumer and common-factor argument used above. A restriction also pays one gate per essential raw input that disappears. To verify this count, trim the original binary circuit to its live cone. With $v$ raw inputs and $s$ gates its underlying connected undirected multigraph has $2s$ edges and cycle rank $s-v+1$. After pinning, propagate constants and copies, pin any semantically irrelevant remaining raw inputs, and trim to a nonconstant output. The simplified network is a graph minor: each bypass contracts an operand edge and drops the other incoming edge, and dead branches are deleted. Cycle rank cannot increase under these operations. With $v'$ inputs and $s'$ gates left, $s'-v'+1\le s-v+1$, as claimed.

The program also excludes impossible first appearances of raw inputs. For a candidate minimum size, the two cofactor lower bounds give the possible numbers of AND and OR readers of each raw variable. Seed the set of seen vertices with every feasible raw-raw pair for each operation. A singly read AND endpoint of such a pair must have its entire neighborhood equal to its partner. A singly read OR endpoint must have its entire neighborhood in the partner's neighborhood. For an unseen input meeting a nonraw sibling, allow a multiple reader to enter freely. A single AND reader may enter when all its neighbors are already seen, and a single OR reader when its independent distance-two representatives can all be selected from seen vertices. Iterate until stable. Every real circuit's input order is contained in this overestimate by the masking argument above. A proper final subset excludes that candidate minimum.

All 66 isomorphism classes on eight nonisolated vertices attain $U$, including all 27 cyclic classes. The same holds for each smaller class. Five of the eight-vertex graphs needed an additional bound. For three graphs the elementary lower bound was ten and the star circuit has eleven gates. The binary monotone circuit encoding with CaDiCaL's internal proof checker excluded ten gates in all three. The program lists their canonical masks and reproduces the three checks with `--solve-eight`. For the other two, relabeling identifies the [theta with three length-three paths](single-read-cover-kernels.md) and the eight-vertex [Petersen deletion](petersen-raw-bound.md). Their existing lower bounds are twelve and thirteen, respectively. A 100-second SAT attempt on each of these last two was inconclusive; the independent lower bounds settle them.

With `--owners`, the script checks actual gate values and strict cones on all sixteen input assignments. The enumeration uses four raw inputs, up to four binary gates, a sorted topological schedule and a full output cone, followed by the two larger controls. It found 5,123 live correct circuits in total, 4,886 pivotal ownerless gate occurrences, 4,164 successful one-edge repairs and 7,009 successful choices of one or two admissible ancestors. Multiple distinct choices may repair the same gate. Every tested rewire preserved the full truth table and every previously owned edge.

## Arbitrarily many vertices with a cover of size four

The desk premise is impossible for **every graph of girth at least five with vertex-cover size at most four**, without assuming bipartiteness. To check the finite reduction, start with a minimum cover $C$ of size $k$. Vertices outside $C$ form an independent set. If two nonisolated vertices have the same open neighborhood, that neighborhood consists of a single vertex by the four-cycle condition. Delete one of the duplicate leaves. This removes one edge and preserves the cover number: a cover of the smaller graph can use the parent instead of the other leaf. Its zero pin in a minimum circuit removes at least one gate, and joining the two leaves by a raw OR restores the old graph at a cost of one. Isolated vertices can be omitted. In the reduced graph there is at most one outside singleton-neighborhood vertex per cover vertex. Each larger outside neighborhood claims a distinct pair of cover vertices. Hence at most $2k+\binom{k}{2}$ vertices remain.

Enumerate all graphs induced on $C$. An outside neighborhood must avoid adjacent pairs in $C$ and pairs with a common neighbor in $C$; two outside neighborhoods may intersect in at most one vertex. Conversely these conditions and the internal girth test exclude every three- and four-cycle. Filter for an actual minimum cover of size $k$ and for the absence of duplicate leaves. The kernel count at $k=1,2,3,4$ is respectively 1, 3, 18 and 186. Among them there are respectively 0, 0, 5 and 96 nonbipartite types. Every nonbipartite type attains $U$ by the restriction calculation above. The [previous bipartite cover-four proof](single-read-cover-kernels.md) supplies the bipartite cases. Duplicate leaves then restore the conclusion without a bound on the original number of vertices.

At cover size five, the same kernel construction gives 4,251 uncolored types. Of its 3,238 nonbipartite types, 2,352 attain the star bound by this calculation. The lower bound falls short by one gate for 804 and by two gates for 82. Run `--covers 5` to reproduce this larger count.

For larger unrestricted covers, the numerical lower equals the star upper on 165 of 179 nine-vertex types, 465 of 576 ten-vertex types and 1,396 of 2,094 eleven-vertex types. The remaining gaps measure the strength of the certificates. None gives a circuit below the star upper. Run the [calculation](edge-owner-exchange.py) with a vertex limit, or with `--covers` for the cover census. The unrestricted graph and circuit inequalities in this note use the mathematical derivations above; the Lean file checks the gate-list exchange, the normalization under the unique-pivot condition, the distinct-pivot obstruction, the two-source truth rule and the local masking lemmas.
