# Four gates for K₂,₃ and the graphs within one gate of their input bound

Case L-meta-2136 asks for at most three binary gates on the six-edge complete bipartite graph K₂,₃. Its exact minimum is **four**. OR the two points, OR the three lines using two gates, then AND the two results. Each of the five inputs is essential, and a binary output cone with three gates has at most four input vertices. The project had already accepted [BastianMIllan's lower bound for the opposite case, L-meta-2135](https://github.com/open-goat/pequalnp-proofs/pull/4632). The [independent Lean development](TightBinaryCircuit.lean) proves the nonexistence statement and verifies a four-gate witness against the project's actual circuit semantics, backward-read condition and all 32 assignments.

For any finite simple graph with n nonisolated vertices and at least one edge, the following gives a complete test for a monotone circuit of at most n gates. The graph function accepts an assignment exactly when both ends of some edge are active. First identify vertices with identical open neighborhoods. Such vertices are independent and called **false twins**. Take one representative per class. Write Pₖ for the path on k vertices.

| Components of the graph after identifying false twins | Minimum binary AND/OR gate count |
| --- | --- |
| Every component is P₂ | n − 1 |
| One component is P₄ or P₅ and every other component is P₂ | n |
| Every other case | At least n + 1 |

Isolated vertices can be ignored. Circuits may use arbitrary fanout. The classification at n gates is a written proof below. The checks in Lean include the tight-circuit reconstruction, the lower bound from three consecutive edges and the lemma about communicating a group of inputs through one bit.

## When every gate is needed just to connect the inputs

Start at the output and walk the gate list backwards, keeping a set of required wires. An unused gate changes nothing. At a used gate replace its output by its operands. A gate of fan-in at most two grows the set by at most one. The final set contains every essential raw input, since two assignments agreeing there give the same output. Thus at least n − 1 gates are needed. At equality each reverse step replaces one required wire by two distinct wires absent from the remaining set. Substituting these pairs into the output expression yields a binary formula in which each input occurs exactly once.

This reasoning also handles constant gates, repeated operands and out-of-range reads under the project's actual false-default evaluation. The Lean theorem `exactExcess` gives an identity. The gate count plus one equals n, plus the number of final required wire indices outside the input range, plus the sum of the shortfalls from a one-wire increase at each reverse step. The theorem `tightCircuit` constructs the read-once expression at zero excess and preserves its truth on every input.

A connected graph with a read-once AND/OR formula is complete bipartite. Its variables on the two sides of any root gate are disjoint. A root OR cannot cover an edge between the sides, since on such an edge each child sees at most a singleton, and the output rejects singletons. At a root AND every edge must cross between the sides since each child is zero on the empty input. Every variable on either side must activate its child on its singleton because that variable has a neighbor. A monotone child accepting all its singletons is the OR of its inputs. The graph therefore contains exactly all cross pairs. More generally, restricting to each connected component leaves a read-once formula after constants are simplified. Conversely each complete bipartite component uses one AND on the disjunctions of its two shores. Their OR is read-once across the whole graph.

There is a local certificate for strict inequality. At a true assignment and a false assignment to a read-once monotone formula, at most one coordinate can be individually pivotal at both. At an OR choose a child true on the first assignment. Any common pivotal variable must belong to that child; the other child is unaffected by changing it and is false on the second assignment. At an AND choose a child false on the second assignment and use the same reasoning with the truth values reversed. Descend to a single leaf. In particular, for edges a–b, b–c and c–d with a–d absent, take the positive input {b,c} and negative input {a,d}. Both b and c are pivotal at both inputs. Every monotone circuit therefore needs at least n gates. The same certificate works for a triangle with a = d. Lean verifies this directly for the project's `graphFun` in `graphPathLower`.

## One more gate allows a single duplicated wire

Consider a minimum circuit of size n that fails the read-once test. Remove unused gates, simplify invalid reads and constants, contract unary gates and collapse equal operands. Any saving would produce a circuit of at most n − 1 gates, already ruled out. Its output cone therefore has n essential sources, n genuine binary gates and 2n operand positions. Each of its 2n − 1 nonoutput vertices must have a reader. Exactly one wire has two readers and every other nonoutput wire has one.

If the duplicated wire is raw, call it v. Otherwise its private upstream tree reads a set S of raw variables. None of these variables has another route out of this tree. The output depends on S through just one Boolean value H. If an edge joins p in S to l outside S, the empty input in S and the singleton p give different values of H: adding exterior singleton l distinguishes them. With the exterior empty, the graph rejects both. An internal edge would need a third value of H because the graph would accept with the exterior empty. Hence S is independent. Every vertex of S has an exterior neighbor, so its singleton gives the same value of H as singleton p. Testing each exterior singleton shows all vertices of S have identical neighborhoods. The positive tree for H is zero at the empty assignment and true on every singleton of S, so H is exactly their OR. This binary-interface argument is formalized in `binaryCutTwins`.

If no edge crosses S, then S is a union of whole components. Set all exterior inputs to zero. The restricted output depends only on H, is monotone in H and realizes a nonconstant graph on S, so it equals H. These components have a read-once formula. Pin H to zero for the component functions outside S; the remaining raw inputs occur once. This makes the entire graph a union of bicliques, contrary to the assumed strict excess. The same argument covers S containing all inputs.

Consequently the only exceptional wire may be treated as a single vertex v, either an original input or one independent class of twins. Unfold the circuit after this identification. The resulting monotone formula reads v twice and each other vertex once. A component excluding v becomes read-once after the outside variables are set to zero, so it must be complete bipartite. Restrict to the component containing v. If it is not complete bipartite, both copies of v survive simplification. Every other vertex in the component is essential and occurs once.

## The connected component containing v

If both copies lie in the same root child, the child supports are disjoint. The same root argument as for a read-once graph applies: an OR disconnects the graph, and an AND gives a complete bipartite graph. Thus in an exceptional connected component, v occurs once in each root child. Their other variables are disjoint sets A and B.

At a root OR, each child computes the graph induced on its own variables. When its variables alone are active, the other child sees at most v, which cannot activate either child because the full graph rejects singleton v. Each child is read-once, so its components are complete bipartite. Connectedness forces every one of these components to contain v, since the two children have no other common vertex and an OR produces no edges across their private sets. Thus this component is two bicliques meeting at v.

At a root AND, at least one child rejects singleton v. If both reject it, v has no neighbor, because one of the two children still sees just v on any incident edge. Suppose therefore that the right child accepts v and the left rejects it. There are no edges within A or within B, and v has no neighbors in B. Let A′ consist of the vertices of A that activate the left child on their singleton. Between A and B, the edges are the product of A′ and the singleton-activating subset of B. Every vertex of B needs a neighbor, hence this subset is all of B. Every vertex of A′ is adjacent to v by monotonicity; each vertex of A outside A′ has no possible neighbor except v and therefore must also be adjacent to v. The whole graph consists of the biclique between A′ and B ∪ {v}, together with the star between v and A outside A′. Empty or redundant pieces give a single biclique. Otherwise these two bicliques again meet only at v.

After identifying false twins, two bicliques meeting only at v have a simple description. On each side of the meeting there is one class of neighbors of v, and possibly one class adjacent only to that neighbor class. If neither private class exists the two neighbor classes coalesce and the quotient is P₂. With one private class it is P₄, and with both it is P₅. Splitting v back into a class of independent twins leaves this quotient unchanged. Every interclass adjacency is complete, so representatives lose no information about adjacency between classes.

Conversely compute the OR of every original twin class, using n − k gates for k classes. P₂ needs one additional gate. P₄ and P₅ have two point centers u,v with a common neighbor class C and at most two exclusive classes A,B. Compute

    (u AND (A OR C)) OR (v AND (B OR C)).

Omit the indicated OR when its exclusive class is empty. This costs four gates on the four classes of P₄ and five on the five classes of P₅. Joining distinct component outputs by OR gives exactly the first two gate counts in the table. The private path of length three witnesses the strict lower bound in the exceptional component.

## Case checks

On K₂,₃ a minimum circuit consists of the point OR, the two line ORs and the final AND. The read-once argument forces that decomposition, since the connected graph has a unique bipartition up to swapping shores. Each child computes the OR of its distinct inputs; a binary AND inside that child would reject a singleton from one of its two nonempty sides. Ignoring operand order but distinguishing topological gate schedules, there are **nine** optimum circuits: three choices of first line pair and three positions for the point OR among the two successive line ORs. With ordered operands there are 144 schedules.

The [finite verifier](two-shore-size.py) checks all 2,880 commutative four-gate schedules permitted by the exact frontier bound, and all 63 nonempty labeled two-by-three graphs. It also compares the classification to independent exhaustive truth-table synthesis on all 814 isolate-free labeled graphs with two through five vertices, including nonbipartite graphs. The one-extra-gate search permits duplication of a raw wire or an internal wire. Run `python3 notes/two-shore-size.py` and, from the project's Lean directory, `lake env lean ../notes/TightBinaryCircuit.lean`.
