# Three-term Boolean matrix product

[L-meta-18919](https://pequalnp.com/lemma#L-meta-18919) asks for a circuit of at most 44 AND, OR and NOT gates for the nine entries of a 3 by 3 Boolean matrix product. **The monotone optimum is exactly 45. Every circuit needs at least 31 binary gates even when complementation is free.** A 44-gate circuit, if it exists, must therefore use between one and thirteen NOTs. We also rule out a saving through just one shared gate whose inputs ultimately come from opposite matrices. The full desk question remains open.

The [replay](matrix-product-18919.py) builds the usual 45-gate circuit. Each output uses three AND gates and two OR gates. A separate oracle takes the set intersection of a three-bit row and a three-bit column. It agreed with all nine output wires on all 262,144 inputs. The desk statement matched the live catalogue, including all eighteen input positions and the 44-gate budget.

## Lower bound for monotone circuits

For a fixed cell t, let its three positive tests be the inputs with just the two bits of one matching lane switched on. Its six negative tests are indexed by the nonempty proper subsets S of the three lane indices. Start with every input switched on. Switch off the bits in t's row on lanes in S, and the bits in t's column on the other lanes. The target is true on all three positive tests and false on all six negative tests.

For a wire, let P be the set of positive tests it accepts, and N the family of negative tests it rejects, represented by their sets S. Write L for the intersection of the sets S in N, and R for the intersection of their complements. An empty family gives all three lanes in each intersection. Count a wire for t when **|P| exceeds both |L| and |R|**. This requires at least one accepted positive and one rejected negative. None of the raw wires qualifies. A raw bit of this cell accepts only its own positive lane, and that lane is in L or R respectively. A raw bit outside this cell accepts none of its positives.

A monotone wire can count for at most one cell. If two cells have different rows and columns, each positive test for one is contained in every negative test for the other, so the wire cannot accept the former and reject the latter. For two distinct cells sharing a row, every positive lane accepted for the second must lie in L for the first. Otherwise its two on-bits survive in a rejected negative test. If the wire counted for both, this would force the second cell to have strictly fewer accepted positive lanes than the first, and by symmetry strictly more. The same argument uses R for cells sharing a column. Direct comparison of the actual 18-bit inputs also excluded all 4,435,236 pairs of qualifying wire patterns at distinct cells.

It remains to show that the output needs at least five counted gates for its cell. On the nine test inputs, a wire is just a nine-bit pattern. In bit order the three positive tests come first, then the negative tests with S encoded by the integers 1 through 6. The output pattern is 7. Grant **every** uncounted pattern as a free wire, regardless of whether any circuit could produce it for free. There are 161 free patterns and 351 counted ones. AND and OR act bitwise.

The replay exhausts all ways to obtain 7 with at most four counted gates, with arbitrary reuse. For the first counted gate, any two free patterns are available, giving 186 possibilities. For the second, also allow the first result. For the third, also allow the second result and the operation on the first two. The fourth gate can combine the third result with any free pattern or either earlier result; a result already available sooner is checked separately. Earlier repeated patterns cannot help. The target never occurs. The replay checks 34,698 ordered initial pairs using sets of possible third and final operands. An independent implementation explicitly checked all 6,497,094 ordered triples of distinct counted results. Intermediate free results are available at every step. Empty or unary AND/OR gates, constants, invalid reads and repeated operands cannot create another pattern beyond those granted or already present.

Each cell therefore needs at least five counted gates. As no monotone wire can count for two cells and no raw input counts, all nine need at least 45. The schoolbook construction attains the bound.

## Removing one complete lane with signs allowed

Allow both signs of every wire and constants for free. Physical NOT gates can be absorbed into signs. Retain only genuine binary gates on two different signals; folds, repeated operands, unused gates and constants cannot reduce our lower bound. Fix a lane and call its three row bits and three column bits the selected bits. When those six are the only nonzero inputs, the output is their Boolean outer product. Its 49 nonzero products are all different, since their active rows and columns recover both three-bit vectors. Zero gives a fiftieth result.

We can fix the selected bits so that this lane vanishes and save **at least eight binary gates**. Any fixed settings with all three bits on one side zero are allowed. Under any such fixing, every direct reader of a selected bit becomes a constant or a signed copy of its other operand. None of the original output gates can be a direct reader: every output remains nonconstant when an individual bit is fixed to either value, including bits outside its cell.

Let m be the number of these readers. All information from the six bits must pass through reader signals, so m is at least six. If m is at least eight the claim follows. If m is seven, take a reader with an outside consumer; at least one must exist. One of its selected bits can be fixed to its controlling value, keeping the opposite side zero. This forces the reader to a constant and its outside consumer folds as well.

Suppose m is six. Each reader value is needed as an input to some gate outside the group: five outgoing bits cannot distinguish 50 products when the other lanes are fixed to zero. No reader can take two distinct selected bits directly. A signed AND or OR of two such bits has a majority value on 48 of the 64 input assignments. At most 16 of the 49 distinct nonzero outer products could lie on the minority side. Hence at least 33 different outputs occur on the majority side, exceeding the 32 possibilities of the other five reader bits. With no two selected bits entering the same reader, each of the six bits has exactly one reader. The three readers for the row bits have at least two distinct outside consumers, by fan-in two. We can force the relevant row readers to constants simultaneously by setting their respective row bits to their controlling values and setting all column bits of the lane to zero. Both consumers fold in addition to the six readers.

All other outputs now compute the two-lane product. Its [signed binary lower bound](matrix-product-17823.md#removing-a-row-or-column-even-with-free-complementation) is 23, so the original circuit needs at least 31 binary gates. This argument permits arbitrary nonmonotone sharing and arbitrary output selectors.

## What one shared mixed gate can save

Even allow arbitrary preprocessing of the whole left matrix and of the whole right matrix separately, including complementation. Call a binary gate mixed when its ancestry includes inputs from both sides. For a given output, stop tracing its mixed cone at its pure-side inputs. The cone must receive at least three distinct signals from each side. The left signals must distinguish the eight possible values of its row, since a right column selecting one lane reads that lane. The same holds for the right signals. A connected cone with six leaves needs at least five binary gates.

At equality the cone is a tree with six distinct leaves, three from each side. Its three left signals give a bijective encoding of that row alone. Indeed, different row values require disjoint sets of possible triples, exhausting all eight triples. The three right signals similarly encode its column alone. Thus minimum cones for distinct outputs cannot share a mixed gate: such a gate has a nonconstant pure ancestor from each side, and at least one of its row and column would have to belong to two different cells on that side.

In fact the encodings at equality are just signed permutations of the raw coordinates. Push complements down the read-once tree. The output is monotone in the resulting signed leaf features. Increasing the three encoded left coordinates can only increase the computed function on every right column; the inverse map from encoded coordinates to row masks therefore preserves inclusion. A bijective order-preserving self-map of a finite poset has an order-preserving inverse, by taking powers of the permutation. The automorphisms of the Boolean lattice permute its three singletons and preserve all their joins. The replay checked all 40,320 encodings, finding exactly the six coordinate permutations after the choice of signs. The right side has the same property.

If only one mixed gate were shared across output cones, let it occur in m cones. At most one of those m could have just five gates. Summing the nine cone sizes gives at least 45+(m−1). Only the shared gate was counted more than once, exactly m−1 times too many. This again requires at least 45 distinct mixed gates. Any smaller circuit must have at least two distinct mixed binary gates shared across output cones.

The 45-gate witness, the monotone exclusion and these bounds on a nonmonotone witness do not establish the assigned 44-gate existence claim.
