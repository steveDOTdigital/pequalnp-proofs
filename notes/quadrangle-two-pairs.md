# Two nested AND pairs on the order-three plane

For the 52 incident pairs and 234 quadrangle inputs in L-meta-1963, a binary monotone separator with at most two ordered pairs of distinct ancestor and descendant AND gates needs at least **13 AND gates**. If it has just 13 AND gates, it needs at least **64 gates in total**. Raw inputs and intermediate gates may be shared arbitrarily. Consequently a counterexample below 64 gates with exactly two nested pairs would need at least 14 ANDs. We also exclude one nested pair regardless of the number of ANDs, all circuits whose two nested pairs have disjoint endpoints, two lower ANDs entering the same port of one maximal AND, and all circuits with one lower AND shared by two maximal ANDs. The desk permits more ANDs and more nested pairs.

## Removing lower ANDs that reject all negative inputs

Trim to the output and simplify constants and unary gates first. Call a gate safe if it rejects every negative input. If a safe gate H feeds a later AND, replace all its uses by zero, retain its original definition, and OR H with the residual output. On positives where H is one the new output is one. Everywhere else on the promise the substitution preserves the output. Constant propagation removes at least one immediate reader of H, paying for the final OR. The new circuit has no more gates or ANDs, and strictly fewer comparable AND pairs when H is an AND. Trimming and simplifying can only discard old paths or shorten them. Repeat until every lower AND is unsafe. This applies also when the original circuit includes unused gates or uses one operand more than once.

With at most two comparable pairs there is no three-AND chain. After this normalization every lower AND is a product of two raw disjunctions. Every maximal AND feeds the output through ORs alone, so is safe. The output may be treated as the union of their accepted flags. A raw disjunct reaching the output would accept a forbidden singleton. Flatten OR paths symbolically; this step changes no gate count. An unsafe lower AND cannot reach both input ports of a maximal AND, since that maximal AND would accept a negative on which the lower AND is true. Empty raw ports in the displayed formulas mean false.

## Covering the flags component by component

Join comparable AND gates in an undirected graph. Its components are singletons, single pairs, or two pairs sharing one end. For each component take the union of the flags accepted by its maximal ANDs. We show that its matching number is at most its number of ANDs.

- A singleton is a safe product of two raw clauses. Its flags form a star.
- A single comparable pair has output of the form `G(X ∨ AB)`, where juxtaposition means AND and capital letters are raw disjunctions. Distributing gives three raw clauses. Its flags lie in a star or a double star with adjacent centers.
- If two lower ANDs feed one maximal AND, they occur on just one port each. On opposite ports, distributing gives at most four raw clauses. On the same port, the expression is `G(X ∨ AB ∨ DE)`, a product of at most five clauses. The matching number is at most three.
- If one lower AND feeds two maximal ANDs, their outputs are `G_i(X_i ∨ AB)` for `i = 1, 2`. Each has matching number at most two. The following joint check shows that their union has matching number at most three, even with arbitrary sharing among the raw disjunctions.

The single-product statements use the [two- and three-clause classification](quadrangle-three-clause.md) and the [four- and five-clause matching bounds](quadrangle-four-clauses.md). For the joint check, suppose four disjoint flags were covered. As neither output covers three, the flags split into two pairs, one per output. Shrink each guard to one endpoint of each of its two flags. Both guards still hit the designated flags, and the set of exposed negatives only shrinks. A flag must then be covered by the shared helper unless one of its endpoints may be included in its branch's raw disjunction without hitting a negative that passes the guard. For each such forced flag both helper operands must contain an endpoint. Shrink each operand to one endpoint per forced flag. No negative passing either guard may hit both operands. Conversely, feasible operands and guards would give a witness: add a safe endpoint for every unforced flag to the relevant raw disjunction.

The [replay](quadrangle-two-pairs.py) tests all three pairings, all minimal guards, and every unordered pair of minimal helper operands. None works on any of the 39 projective orbits of four-matchings, accounting for all 120,627 matchings. It uses the actual quadrangle rows, including the distinction between points and lines.

The incidence graph is bipartite, so each component's accepted flags have a vertex cover of its matching size. The union of these covers covers all 52 flags and needs at least 13 vertices since the graph is four-regular. Thus the circuit has at least 13 ANDs.

## Equality with 13 AND gates

If there are only 13 ANDs after normalization, every component must attain its whole AND budget, and its chosen cover must be disjoint from the other chosen covers. Every edge has exactly one endpoint among the 13 chosen vertices, by equality in the degree count. Connectedness forces the chosen shore to be all points or all lines. Each component must therefore accept all four flags of each of its chosen centers, as no other component can cover them.

A single comparable pair cannot cover two full stars on the same shore. In a double star only one vertex on a given shore can have degree greater than one. Likewise a common lower AND feeding two outputs cannot cover three full stars: outside the two exceptional vertices on that shore, each vertex has degree at most two in the union.

For the remaining shape, a single output with two lower ANDs is a product of at most five raw clauses. For each of the 286 triples of points and each of the 286 triples of lines the replay verifies that the three full stars contain a matching requiring at least six clauses. For each three-matching orbit it uses the exhaustive canonical clause-cover calculation, and records the triples reached by projective images of matchings with minimum at least six. Both lists exhaust their shore. Thus this shape also cannot attain its AND budget.

Only singleton components remain. The normalized circuit is flat and needs at least 64 gates by the [shared-OR preprocessing bound](quadrangle-three-clause.md). Normalization could not have increased its size. The ordinary construction with 13 four-edge stars has 39 neighbor ORs, 13 ANDs and 12 output ORs, attaining both counts.

## A size bound independent of the number of ANDs

With one comparable pair and unsafe lower AND `H = AB`, let `G` be the raw guard at its only maximal AND. Clamp H to zero everywhere downstream and retain the original H for a new output term `HG`. The residual is flat. The added AND and final OR cost at most two gates, and at least one old immediate reader disappears. The transformed circuit consists of ordinary products and one three-clause product with a private inner AND. It needs at least 65 gates by the [single three-clause term bound](quadrangle-three-clause.md). If the extra term is redundant, discard it and its private gates and apply the 64-gate flat bound. Hence the original needed at least 64. The safe-lower case was already handled by normalization.

For a common unsafe lower AND feeding two maximal ANDs, retain H and append `H(G₁ ∨ G₂)` to the clamped flat output. This costs at most three gates. If H has at least two distinct immediate readers in the trimmed circuit, at least two gates disappear on clamping. Thus the same bound of 64 follows, regardless of the number of other ANDs and the extent of OR sharing. If both maximal ANDs use the same guard wire, only two new gates are needed and one disappearing reader suffices. If clamping kills the entire residual output, the extra term by itself would have to cover all 52 flags, which its matching bound excludes.

Even when only one reader disappears, the transformation increases size by at most two. It proves 63 unconditionally for this common-helper shape, and 64 when the extra three-clause term is a genuine double star, since in that case the transformed bound is 66.

## Two disjoint nested pairs, with arbitrary raw OR sharing

The needed refinement of the [three-clause counting proof](quadrangle-three-clause.md) is that two three-clause terms with **distinct private inner AND gates** require at least 66 gates together with the ordinary terms of a separator. Here a private inner AND feeds only its own outer AND. First remove redundant terms. Deleting a redundant three-clause term frees its two private ANDs, so the 64-gate bound for the remaining terms already suffices. Ordinary terms redundant on the positive flags can also be dropped.

Retain both three-clause terms. Use the counting proof's notation: r ordinary products, e three-clause stars, k genuine double stars, d distinct central edges of genuine double stars, and s distinct private inner ANDs. Here e+k = s = 2. When the OR gates charged by the center forests are distinct across centers, the bound is 51+d+r+e+s gates. Indeed at least 52+d neighbor incidences are counted using at most r+e+2k forest roots. Then add r+e+k+s ANDs and at least r+e+k−1 output ORs.

If both three-clause terms are stars, the chosen forest roots can avoid every double charge. For stars centered on the same shore choose missed clauses whose support on the opposite shore consists only of neighbors, as in the cited counting proof. A gate charged at both centers would require two common neighbors. For stars centered on opposite shores choose the other available kind of missed clause, containing at most one input outside the neighborhood of its center. A gate charged at both centers would contain two distinct point neighbors and two distinct line neighbors, violating that limit. For a single-edge star use its neighbor literal. If the stars have the same center use a single center forest. An ordinary pure forest root cannot double-charge with any of these choices: a pure root contains only neighbors of its center, at most one of which is a neighbor of another center. Thus the bound is 51+r+e+2, at least 66 because r+e star centers cover all flags.

If one term is a star and one a genuine double star, there is at most one impure star center and the ordinary distinct-charge argument applies. Here d=e=1. The two adjacent double-star centers and the other r+1 centers give a mixed cover of size at most r+3. Every mixed cover needs at least 14, so r is at least 11 and 51+d+r+e+s is at least 66. If both terms are genuine double stars, the [pure-arm argument](quadrangle-three-clause.md) already proves 66: a mixed clause requires a gate outside all charged pure cones.

Now suppose the two comparable pairs have disjoint endpoints. Apply safe-gate removal first; if a pair disappears the single-pair result suffices. Otherwise each unsafe lower AND feeds exactly one maximal AND, through only one of its ports. Clamp both lower ANDs to zero while retaining their definitions. At least two different immediate readers disappear, since a common reader would give one maximal AND both lower ancestors. Add back each lower AND conjoined with its own raw guard, and OR the two resulting terms with the flat output, costing at most four gates. Each added term has a distinct private inner AND. The transformed circuit has size at most the old size plus two and needs at least 66 gates. The original needs at least 64. If the residual output vanishes, two three-clause terms could cover a matching of size at most four and cannot separate the full plane.

## Two lower ANDs entering the same port

The same bound of 64 holds when two different unsafe lower ANDs both enter the same port of one maximal AND. Write that term as `G(X ∨ H₁ ∨ H₂)` and clamp both lower ANDs to zero. At least two old gates disappear. If the two lower ANDs have different immediate readers, these both simplify. If their only reader is the same gate, it must be the OR of the two lower ANDs, as both are entering the same port. This OR becomes zero and its immediate reader also simplifies. The guard G has no lower AND ancestor. Retain both lower definitions and add back `GH₁` and `GH₂` as separate terms. At most four gates are added, and the two new triple products have private distinct inner ANDs. The 66-gate bound again gives 64 for the starting circuit. The same matching argument excludes an empty residual.

## The remaining shared-helper case

Suppose the original circuit has at most 63 gates, the lower AND H has just one immediate reader, and H reaches two different maximal ANDs. Its reader K is an OR with a second input independent of H. A maximal AND could not be this sole reader because neither maximal AND is an ancestor of the other. Replace K by that second input everywhere in the residual, removing exactly one gate. Both original maximal ANDs remain as ordinary products with their original guard wires. Append the one three-clause term `H(G₁ ∨ G₂)` as above. The resulting circuit has at most 65 gates. If the new product is redundant, removing its private ANDs would give a flat separator with at most 63 gates. A genuine double star would require 66. Thus its accepted flags form an indispensable star.

No ordinary term can be redundant: deleting a redundant ordinary AND leaves the private three-clause term in a separator with at most 64 gates, whereas such a circuit costs at least 65 (and deleting a redundant triple still costs its two private ANDs). For one triple star the distinct-charge bound above is 51+r+1+1, where r counts ordinary terms. At most 65 gates permits at most twelve ordinary terms, and covering the plane requires at least thirteen stars in total. Hence the twelve ordinary terms and the extra star cover exactly thirteen centers. The equality argument shows that all are distinct, on the same shore, and each term accepts its full four-edge star.

Let c be the center of the extra star, and let s₁,s₂ be the distinct centers at the two original maximal ANDs after substitution. A safe ordinary product accepting the full star at s has raw operands exactly the singleton s and its four neighbors. Indeed, s must occur on one side and its neighbors on the other, since singletons are forbidden. Any other input on the neighbor side would form a forbidden pair with s. An extra input on the singleton side would have to be incident with every neighbor of s, also impossible. Consequently each guard Gᵢ is either the singleton sᵢ or its neighborhood. On the four flags centered at c, the singleton guard is always false. A neighborhood guard can hit only the one common neighbor of sᵢ and c. The disjunction of the two guards therefore hits at most two of the four flags, contradicting the full star at c. This excludes every common-helper shape under 64 gates.

For exactly two comparable AND pairs, after safe-gate removal the only unresolved shape below 64 is now two distinct unsafe lower ANDs reaching **opposite** ports of the same maximal AND, with at least fourteen ANDs in total. Three or more comparable pairs are also outside the result.

These are partial lower bounds. In particular the thirteen-AND argument does not cover a circuit with fourteen ANDs and arbitrary nesting. Verification uses the mathematical argument and the finite Python replay.
